Guides · Online therapy

Is online therapy effective?

The short answer is yes — for many people, and for many concerns, video therapy works about as well as sitting in the same room. The longer answer matters more: it depends on what you are working on, how the sessions are set up, and whether the format fits your life.

What online therapy actually is

Online therapy is real therapy, held over secure video instead of in an office.

You meet the same therapist, at a set time, for a full session — usually 50 minutes for individual therapy. You talk, you work through things, you practise new ways of coping and understanding yourself. The only difference is the room: yours, instead of theirs. Sessions at this practice run on JaneApp, a secure video platform built for healthcare, and you join from wherever you have privacy and a decent connection.

It is not a chat service, and it is not a self-help app. It is a live, one-to-one conversation with a registered therapist, with the same structure and confidentiality as an in-person session.

What video therapy is good for

Most of the work people bring to therapy translates well to video.

Anxiety, depression, grief, stress, burnout, low self-esteem, and life transitions are all commonly worked on online, and for many people the results are comparable to in-person work. The reason is simple: most of therapy is conversation. The healing happens in what is said, what is noticed, and what is understood together — and none of that needs a shared room.

Trauma work can also be done online, including EMDR. That surprises people, because EMDR is often pictured as a therapist waving a hand in front of your face. Over video, the same bilateral stimulation happens on screen, or through tapping, and the phases of the work are the same. You can read more in What is EMDR?

There is also something video does better than an office, for some people. If leaving the house is hard — because of anxiety, exhaustion, a busy schedule, or living far from services — the fact that therapy comes to you can be the difference between getting help and not getting it. Being in your own space can make it easier to be honest, too. Many people find they open up faster from their own couch than from a stranger’s office chair.

Where in-person may be preferred

Honesty cuts both ways, so here is the other side.

Online therapy is not the right fit for everyone, and it is not the right fit for every situation. If you are in crisis, or if your safety is at risk, you need in-person support and crisis services — not a video call. This practice is not a crisis service. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in Canada, or call 911 in an emergency.

Some people simply do better face to face. If you have no private space at home, an unreliable internet connection, or a deep discomfort with video calls, the format itself becomes a barrier, and therapy should not have to fight its own format. Some kinds of work — especially work that depends on reading subtle body language, or on being physically present with someone in a hard moment — can also feel thinner on screen.

And some concerns need more than weekly video sessions: severe symptoms, complex situations, or anything that needs a team of professionals around you. A good therapist will tell you when that is the case, and help you think about what would serve you better. That conversation is exactly what the free consultation is for.

What makes online therapy work

When video therapy works well, it is usually because of a few ordinary things done right.

  • A private space. A room with a door that closes, where you will not be overheard or interrupted. Therapy needs privacy to be honest, and honesty is where the work happens.
  • A good connection. Stable internet and a device with a camera and microphone. You do not need fancy equipment — a phone, tablet, or laptop is enough — but frozen video and dropped audio wear down a session fast.
  • The same therapist, every session. Therapy works through relationship. Seeing the same person each time means they learn how you think, what your patterns are, and when something has shifted. At this practice, that is always the case: one clinician, Yeukai Tsikira, from your first session onward.
  • Showing up consistently. Weekly sessions build momentum in a way that occasional ones do not. The work between sessions — noticing, practising, reflecting — matters as much as the hour itself.
  • Saying what is actually true. The format does not change this. The sessions that help most are the ones where you say the thing you were not sure you would say.

None of this is complicated. But it is worth setting up properly, because the format should disappear into the background and leave just you and the conversation.

Who online therapy suits

It suits people who want therapy to fit their life, not the other way around.

If you live somewhere with few therapists nearby, work irregular hours, care for children or parents, or simply prefer your own space, virtual sessions remove the travel, the waiting room, and the scheduling puzzle. It also suits people across Canada equally — this practice works with clients in every province and territory, in English and French, across time zones.

It suits teenagers too, in many cases. Teens aged 12 and up can do video sessions from home, which often feels less intimidating than an office visit. Parents are involved in setting it up, and the young person gets a space of their own. You can read more on the individual therapy page and the teen therapy information on this site.

It may suit you less if you have no private space, if video calls drain you, or if you know yourself well enough to say you need to be in a room with someone. That is useful information, not a failure. The consultation is a good place to say it out loud and think it through together.

Is online therapy as effective as in-person therapy?

For many people and many concerns — anxiety, depression, grief, stress, and life transitions — research suggests video therapy works about as well as in-person therapy. It is not the right fit for everyone or every situation, and a consultation is the best way to judge whether it fits yours.

Is online therapy private and secure?

Sessions here run on JaneApp, a secure video platform built for healthcare. Your therapist is bound by the same confidentiality rules as in an office. Your part is a private space and a device only you can access.

What do I need for a video session?

A phone, tablet, or laptop with a camera and microphone, a stable internet connection, and a private room where you will not be interrupted. No special software or equipment is needed — you join through a link.

What happens if my internet drops during a session?

You rejoin through the same link, and the session continues. If the connection cannot be recovered, your therapist will be in touch to reschedule the remaining time. A dropped call is an interruption, not a lost session.

Can I do EMDR online?

Yes. EMDR works over video, using eye movements on screen or tapping, and the phases of the work are the same as in person. Your therapist will talk with you about whether online EMDR is a good fit and how it will be set up. Read the full guide at What is EMDR?
