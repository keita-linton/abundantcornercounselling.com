What is EMDR?

EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing. It is a structured, evidence-based approach for trauma and PTSD — and for many people, it helps with anxiety, depression, and grief too. This guide explains it in plain language: what happens in a session, what the eight phases are, and how it works over video.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in Canada, or call 911 in an emergency. This practice is not a crisis service. EMDR is a therapy for processing painful experiences over time — it is not emergency support.

EMDR in plain language

Some memories do not fade the way others do. They stay raw.

Most of the time, your brain processes what happens to you — it files the experience away, and the feelings attached to it soften. But sometimes a painful or frightening experience does not get filed properly. It stays stuck, and when something reminds you of it, the feelings come back almost as strongly as the first time. A sound, a smell, a place, or a date on the calendar can bring it all back.

EMDR is a way of helping the brain finish that filing. In a session, you bring a painful memory to mind while also following a pattern of bilateral stimulation — usually eye movements, side to side, or tapping on your knees or shoulders, or alternating tones. The stimulation itself is simple. What matters is what happens while it is going on: the memory starts to lose its charge. For many people, the memory stays — but it stops hijacking the present.

That is the honest version. EMDR is a well-established, evidence-based approach for trauma and PTSD, and research suggests it helps many people. It is not magic, it is not a quick fix, and it does not work the same way for everyone. What it offers is a structured way of working through experiences that talking alone has not shifted.

The eight phases, in plain language

EMDR follows a clear structure. Your therapist will move through these phases with you — not in one session, but across the course of the work.

  1. History and planning. You and your therapist talk about your history and what you want to work on. Together you decide which memories to focus on, and in what order. Nothing is rushed into processing before you are ready.
  2. Preparation. You learn grounding and calming techniques — ways to steady yourself when feelings run high. This phase matters as much as any other, because the work ahead asks something of you, and you need tools in hand before you begin.
  3. Assessment. For the memory you are working on, you identify the image that captures it, the belief about yourself that goes with it, and the feelings and body sensations attached to it. This gives the work a clear target.
  4. Desensitization. This is the part most people picture when they think of EMDR. You hold the memory in mind while following the eye movements or tapping, in sets. Between sets, you notice what comes up — thoughts, feelings, images — and report it briefly. Over the sets, the distress attached to the memory usually lessens.
  5. Installation. Once the distress has eased, you strengthen a more helpful belief about yourself — the one you would rather carry. The same eye movements or tapping help that belief feel true, not just known.
  6. Body scan. You check in with your body. Trauma lives in the body as much as in the mind, and any tension or discomfort that is still attached to the memory gets noticed and worked with.
  7. Closure. Every session ends with care. If the work is not finished, your therapist helps you put it down safely — with grounding techniques and a plan for the days between sessions — so you leave steady.
  8. Reevaluation. At the start of the next session, you check in together: how did the week go, what shifted, what still needs work? The plan adjusts from there.

Not every session touches every phase. Early sessions are mostly history and preparation. Processing sessions focus on phases three to seven. The structure is there to keep the work safe and steady, not to rush it.

What a session feels like

An EMDR session is quieter than most people expect.

You will talk, but not constantly. There are stretches where you follow the eye movements or tapping in silence, noticing what comes up, and then you tell your therapist briefly — a sentence or two — before the next set. You do not have to describe the painful memory in detail. In fact, one of the things people find relieving about EMDR is that it does not require you to retell everything, over and over, the way some therapies do.

The feelings can be strong. That is normal, and it is why preparation comes first. Your therapist is there the whole time, watching for signs that you need a pause, a grounding exercise, or a slower pace. You are in control of the process: if something feels like too much, you say so, and the work stops or slows. A session ends with closure, so you leave settled rather than raw.

After a session, some people feel tired, or notice dreams, or find that things keep shifting for a day or two. Others feel lighter almost at once. Both are normal. Your therapist will talk with you about looking after yourself between sessions.

EMDR online

Yes — EMDR works over video, and the phases are the same.

Over a secure video call, the bilateral stimulation happens on screen — your eyes follow a moving point — or through tapping on your own knees or shoulders, guided by your therapist. The conversation, the preparation, the processing, and the closure all happen the same way they would in an office. What you need is a private space, a stable connection, and a device with a camera, so your therapist can see how you are doing through the session.

Online EMDR suits many people, including those who live far from a trained EMDR therapist or who feel safer doing this work from their own home. It is not right for everyone — if your situation needs in-person support, or if your connection and privacy cannot be relied on, your therapist will say so honestly. That is a conversation for the free consultation, and for the preparation phase, before any processing begins.

Who EMDR helps

EMDR was developed for trauma and PTSD, and that is where the evidence is strongest.

It is used most often for post-traumatic stress — after accidents, assault, loss, childhood experiences, or other events that left a lasting mark. But it is also used for anxiety, depression, and grief, especially when those feelings are tied to specific experiences that have not resolved on their own. If your anxiety has a root — a moment, or a pattern of moments, where it started — EMDR may help you work on that root rather than only managing the symptoms.

That fits how this practice works overall. The framework here is root-cause healing: we work on where the pain started, not just how to cope with it. EMDR is one of the ways that work happens, alongside talk therapy approaches like Trauma-Focused CBT, Internal Family Systems, Somatic Therapy, and ACT. Which approach fits is something you and your therapist decide together, in the consultation and the early sessions.

Readiness and safety

EMDR is powerful work, and it should not be rushed.

Not everyone starts processing in the first few sessions. Some people need time in the preparation phase first — building grounding skills, stabilising daily life, and making sure there is enough support around them. That is not a delay. It is part of the therapy, and it is what makes the processing safe when it begins.

Your therapist will also be honest with you about whether EMDR is the right approach right now. If you are in crisis, if your living situation is unsafe, or if there are concerns that need other kinds of support first, processing may wait. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in Canada, or call 911 in an emergency.

What EMDR is not

A few honest corrections, because EMDR is widely misunderstood.

  • It is not hypnosis. You are awake, aware, and in control throughout. Nobody is putting you into a trance.
  • It does not erase memories. The memory stays. What changes is how much power it has over you — how strongly it pulls you back, and how much it shapes what you believe about yourself.
  • It is not a quick fix. Some people notice a shift after a few processing sessions. Others need more time. There is no set number of sessions, and no outcome is guaranteed.
  • It is not the only way. EMDR is one approach among several. If it does not fit you, or if the work goes better another way, your therapist will say so and adjust — including working through individual therapy instead.

Does EMDR work online?

Yes. EMDR works over video, using eye movements on screen or tapping, and the phases of the work are the same as in person. You need a private space, a stable connection, and a device with a camera so your therapist can see how you are doing.

Will I have to describe the trauma in detail?

No. EMDR does not require you to retell everything in detail, the way some therapies do. You identify the memory, the belief attached to it, and what you feel — and much of the processing happens quietly, with brief check-ins between sets.

How many EMDR sessions will I need?

There is no set number. Some people notice a shift after a few processing sessions; others need more time, especially if there are several memories to work on or a longer preparation phase is needed. You and your therapist review progress together as you go.

Is EMDR safe?

EMDR is a well-established, evidence-based approach, and safety is built into its structure — preparation and grounding come before any processing, and every session ends with closure. Your therapist will talk with you about whether it is the right approach for you right now, and you can pause or stop the work at any time.

What if EMDR brings up strong feelings?

Strong feelings during processing are normal. Your therapist is with you throughout, watching for signs you need a pause or a grounding exercise, and you are in control of the pace. Sessions end with closure so you leave steady, and your therapist will talk with you about looking after yourself between sessions.
