How much does therapy cost in Canada?

Therapy in Canada typically costs $120 to $250 an hour. That is a wide range, and it leaves the real question unanswered: what will it cost you, for the kind of help you need? This guide explains what sets the price, what this practice charges, and how receipts, insurance, and free options work.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in Canada, or call 911 in an emergency. This practice is not a crisis service. If cost is the barrier between you and help right now, the free options near the end of this guide are a good place to start.

The short answer

Most private therapy in Canada falls between $120 and $250 per hour.

A standard individual session — usually 50 minutes — commonly lands somewhere in that band. Longer sessions cost more, and some kinds of therapy cost more than others. A family session, which runs longer and involves more people, will usually be priced above a standard individual session. A specialised session like EMDR, which runs 90 minutes, will usually be priced higher still.

That range is a guide, not a rule. Prices vary by province, by the therapist’s training and registration, and by whether the practice has an office or works online. What matters most is not the number on its own, but what you get for it — and whether the cost is sustainable for you over weeks and months, not just for one session.

What affects the price

Five things mostly decide what a session costs.

  • Session length. A 50-minute session costs less than a 75- or 90-minute one, because you are paying for the therapist’s time. Longer sessions exist for a reason — family work and EMDR need the extra room — but the price follows the clock.
  • Type of therapy. Individual therapy is the baseline. Family therapy involves more people and more complexity. EMDR is a specialised, structured approach that takes longer sessions and specific training, and is priced accordingly.
  • Training and registration. A registered psychotherapist or certified counsellor has completed a master’s-level education, supervised practice, and ongoing professional requirements. That training is part of what the fee reflects — and it is also what makes a receipt eligible for many insurance plans.
  • Where the practice is based. Fees tend to run higher in large cities, where office rents and living costs are higher. A virtual-only practice has lower overhead, which is one reason its fees can sit at the lower end of the range.
  • What is included. A fee usually covers the session itself and the therapist’s preparation and notes. At this practice, it also includes a receipt after every paid session, which you can submit to your insurance provider.

None of these factors says anything about whether a particular therapist is right for you. Price is a practical question, and it deserves a practical answer — but fit matters more than a bargain.

Receipts and insurance

Most people who claim therapy through insurance do it the same way.

After every paid session, you receive a receipt. You submit that receipt to your insurance provider or employee benefits plan, and they reimburse you according to your plan’s coverage for psychotherapy or counselling. Coverage varies a lot from plan to plan — some cover a set amount per session, some cover a yearly total, some cover registered psychotherapists but not other titles. The honest step is to check your own plan before you start, and to ask your insurer two questions: is a Registered Psychotherapist (Qualifying) covered, and what is the per-session or yearly limit?

Two things to know plainly. First, this practice does not offer direct billing — you pay at the time of your session and claim the cost back yourself. Second, Yeukai Tsikira is a Registered Psychotherapist (Qualifying) with the College of Registered Psychotherapists of Ontario, registration 18718, and a Canadian Certified Counsellor with the Canadian Counselling and Psychotherapy Association, member 11246478. Those credentials are what most plans look for, and you can verify them on the CRPO public register and the CCPA find-a-counsellor directory.

Some employers also offer an Employee Assistance Program, which may include a number of free sessions with a different provider. That is worth checking too — it is separate from this practice, but it can be a useful first step.

Free and low-cost options

If $120 a session is out of reach right now, you still have options. None of these is a replacement for ongoing therapy, but all of them are real help.

  • Call or text 988 — Canada’s Suicide Prevention Helpline, free and available 24/7. For anyone in distress, not only for people in immediate crisis.
  • Kids Help Phone, 1-800-668-6868, or text CONNECT to 686868 — free, confidential support for young people across Canada, 24/7.
  • BounceBack — a free, CBT-based programme available in Ontario and British Columbia, with coaching support by phone or video. It is not available in other provinces.
  • 211 — dial 2-1-1 to find community and social services near you, including low-cost and sliding-scale counselling options in many cities. 211 services exist across Canada, including 211 Alberta, 211 Saskatchewan, and 211 Ontario.
  • Community counselling centres — many towns and cities have non-profit counselling services that charge less than private practice, sometimes on a sliding scale based on income. Your local 211 service can point you to what is near you.

If cost is the main thing standing between you and therapy, say so in your free consultation. The $50 promotion spots exist for exactly that reason, and if none is available, Yeukai can help you think about what else might work. Cost should be a conversation, not a closed door.

Working out what it will cost you

Before you book, it helps to do a small piece of arithmetic — honestly, not optimistically.

Think in months, not sessions. Weekly individual therapy at $120 is about $480 a month. At the $50 promotion rate, it is about $200 a month. Then subtract whatever your insurance plan reimburses. Some people find their plan covers most of the cost; others find it covers a few sessions a year. Either way, knowing the number before you start means you can plan for it, instead of discovering it halfway through.

It is also worth asking yourself what you are comparing the cost to. Therapy is not cheap, and this guide will not pretend otherwise. But many people find that the cost of not dealing with anxiety, grief, or burnout — in sleep, in work, in relationships — is higher, and harder to see. That is not a sales argument. It is simply the other side of the same calculation, and it belongs in the decision too.

The work itself follows this practice’s framework of root-cause healing: we work on where the pain started, not just how to cope with it. That matters for cost as well, because work that gets to the root tends to hold — and holding is what makes therapy a season of your life, not a permanent bill. The goal is to move from survival to thriving, and then to need us less.
